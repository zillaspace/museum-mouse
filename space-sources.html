A little context
for our place in space

Astronomical snapshot: 18 September 2026 at 12:00 UTC

The gold pins belong to one small planet. Moving away from it reveals the space between Earth and the Moon, then the much larger distances between the planets. This view uses real astronomical positions and physical radii, with a camera you can move.

What the zoom controls do

The plus and minus buttons, mouse wheel and pinch gesture move the viewpoint closer to or farther from Earth. The angle of view gradually widens from about 37 to 60 degrees on the outward journey. That change in the lens moves the stars across the screen; their parallax from travelling within the solar system is much smaller. Dragging changes the direction from which you look back at Earth.

“Earth & Moon” chooses a view from the side of the line between them. “Solar system” takes the camera about 110 astronomical units from Earth. An astronomical unit, or AU, is 149,597,870.7 km. “World view” returns to the museum globe.

How much would you really see?

At this snapshot the centres of Earth and the Moon are about 403,966 km apart, roughly 32 Earth diameters. The Moon therefore does not sit just beside a large globe. At the scale needed to see the solar system, most planets occupy less than a pixel.

Small outlined rings and readable names identify bodies that would otherwise disappear on a screen. Those are enlarged location markers; the physical discs and distances retain their scale. A body behind Earth can be hidden by it. The Moon’s illuminated side follows the direction of the Sun.

This is an educational astronomical view. Earth keeps the museum atlas’s existing imagery and lighting. Planet colours, simplified spherical shading and star brightness are illustrative. It is not a photograph, and it is not live spacecraft telemetry. The date remains fixed as you explore.

The positions and sources

  • NASA/JPL Horizons supplies the Sun, Moon and seven other planets, with Earth as the coordinate origin. The snapshot uses simultaneous geometric positions, with no light travel time or apparent sky corrections.
  • JPL planetary physical parameters and the satellite tables supply representative spherical radii. The Sun uses the IAU nominal solar radius of 695,700 km.
  • David Nash’s HYG v4.1 catalogue supplies 8,920 stars through visual magnitude 6.5, drawing on Hipparcos, Yale Bright Star and Gliese data. Catalogue motion is applied to the snapshot date. Distances with uncertain or missing parallax remain approximate.
  • IERS Earth orientation records and the IAU SOFA model, through ERFA, align the celestial coordinates with the globe. They account for Earth’s rotation, precession, nutation and polar motion.
About the rendering and precision

The model uses a spherical Earth of radius 6,371.0088 km, consistent with the map engine. Beyond its minimum map zoom, the already distant globe image is reduced to the physical angular size while the surrounding bodies continue through the same perspective calculation. This preserves the existing museum globe; its surface texture is not recomputed from a spacecraft camera.

The body positions are in kilometres. Star distances are in parsecs. Sphere rendering simplifies oblateness, surface features and rings, and does not simulate a camera’s exposure or optics beyond perspective. It omits relativistic light deflection, observer aberration and light travel time. It is suitable for exploring relative scale, not for navigation or planning an observation.
